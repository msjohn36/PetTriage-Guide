Know When It’s an Emergency for Your Pet

Quickly identify warning signs and understand whether your pet needs immediate ER care or can safely wait for a regular vet visit.

Emergency Guide

Use our interactive guide to determine if your pet’s symptoms require emergency care.

Vomiting & Diarrhea

Is your pet vomiting or having diarrhea?

Duration/Frequency

Symptoms persist for more than 24 hours (or 12 hours in young/elderly). Multiple episodes within a few hours.

Blood Present

Vomit or diarrhea appears red, black, or tarry.

Behavior Changes

Significant lethargy, weakness, or collapse.

Appetite/Water Intake

Refusal to eat or drink water or keep water down.

Pain/Discomfort

Distended abdomen, whining, or "praying position".

Retching

Repeated attempts to vomit without producing anything.

What should you do?

See Your Regular Vet

  • Symptoms resolve within 24 hours (or 12 hours in young/elderly).
  • No blood present.
  • Pet remains active and alert.
  • Pet continues to eat and drink water.
  • No signs of pain or discomfort.
  • No retching.

Go to Emergency Vet Immediately

  • Significant lethargy, weakness, or collapse.
  • Refusal to eat or drink water or keep water down.
  • Signs of pain or discomfort.
  • Repeated retching without producing anything.
  • Your regular vet is not available or has closed for the day.
  • You are unsure about the severity of your pet’s condition.

Breathing Difficulties

Is your pet having trouble breathing?

Open-Mouth Breathing/Panting

Constant panting in dogs when not hot/active, or any open-mouth breathing in cats.

Gum Color Changes

Blue, gray, purple, or pale gums/tongue, indicating lack of oxygen.

Abdominal Effort

The belly or chest is working hard to pull air in, creating a "heaving" motion.

Noisy Breathing

Wheezing, whistling, or loud, harsh sounds.

Extended Neck/Hunched Body

Extending the neck out to breathe or crouching in a hunched position.

Rapid Resting Rate

Breathing more than 30 breaths per minute while sleeping or resting.

Lethargy or Collapse

Sudden weakness, fainting, or refusing to move.

Constant Coughing:

Persistent coughing or choking-like spells.

What should you do?

See Your Regular Vet

  • Respiratory rate is slightly elevated but generally under 30 breaths per minute at rest.
  • No labored breathing or open-mouth breathing at rest.
  • No repeated gagging, choking sounds, or coughing fits.
  • Gums and tongue are normal color.
  • Pet is alert, comfortable, and behaving normally.

Go to Emergency Vet Immediately

  • Respiratory rate is consistently above 30 breaths per minute at rest, especially if increasing.
  • Labored breathing, open-mouth breathing, or using abdominal muscles to breathe.
  • Repeated gagging, choking sounds, or severe coughing episodes.
  • Pale, blue, gray, or very dark red gums or tongue.
  • Weakness, collapse, or extreme distress.

Seizures

Is your pet having seizures?

Duration

The seizure lasts longer than 5 minutes.

Frequency

More than one seizure occurs in 24 hours (Cluster Seizures).

Recovery

The pet remains disoriented, blind, or weak for over 30 minutes afterward.

First Time

The very first seizure your pet has ever had.

Appearance

The seizure is accompanied by extreme distress, trouble breathing, or turning blue.

Suspected Causes

Seizures following potential toxin ingestion, head trauma, or heatstroke.

Patient Age

Seizures in very young puppies or seniors with no prior history.

What should you do?

See Your Regular Vet

  • Single seizure lasting less than 2–3 minutes.
  • Pet has a known history of seizures and this episode is typical for them.
  • Pet recovers fully within 15–30 minutes and returns to normal behavior..
  • No injuries occurred during the seizure.
  • Pet is otherwise alert, responsive, and stable after the episode.

Go to Emergency Vet Immediately

  • Seizure lasts longer than 5 minutes (status epilepticus).
  • Multiple seizures occur without full recovery in between (cluster seizures).
  • Two or more seizures occur within a 24-hour period.
  • First-time seizure with no known history.
  • Prolonged disorientation, inability to stand, or abnormal behavior lasting more than 30–60 minutes.
  • Suspected toxin exposure (e.g., chocolate, medications, marijuana, chemicals).
  • Recent head trauma or injury.
  • Very young, senior, or medically compromised pet.
  • Difficulty breathing, collapse, or unresponsiveness after the seizure.

Heatstroke

Is your pet showing signs of heatstroke?

Initial Warning Signs

Excessive, relentless panting; rapid breathing; thick drooling/saliva.

Physical Changes

Gums turning bright red, blue, or purple; dark tongue; hot skin.

Behavioral Changes

Sudden lethargy, weakness, unwillingness to move, confusion, or agitation.

Severe Symptoms

Unsteady gait (wobbling), inability to stand, collapse, vomiting, or diarrhea (sometimes bloody).

Critical Emergencies

Seizures, coma, or unconsciousness.

Heatstroke in dogs and cats is a fatal emergency occurring when body temperatures exceed 104 degrees F.

What should you do?

See Your Regular Vet

  • Mild panting after heat exposure that improves quickly once cooled.
  • Pet remains alert, responsive, and able to walk normally.
  • Normal gum color (pink) and no signs of distress.
  • Symptoms resolve within a short period after rest and cooling.

Go to Emergency Vet Immediately

  • Excessive, relentless panting or rapid breathing that does not improve.
  • Thick drooling or excessive saliva.
  • Gums that are bright red, pale, blue, or purple.
  • Weakness, lethargy, confusion, agitation, or unwillingness to move.
  • Unsteady gait, inability to stand, or collapse.
  • Vomiting or diarrhea (especially if bloody).
  • Seizures, coma, or unconsciousness.
  • Body temperature above 104°F or suspected overheating.
